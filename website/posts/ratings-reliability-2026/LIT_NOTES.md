# Literature check: reviewer agreement and interval overconfidence (for the Unjournal ratings report)

Compiled 2026-10-16. Method: web search plus fetching abstracts/full texts. Where I read the primary PDF/page text myself I say "read"; where the number came only through a search-tool summary or a later paper's description I say "second-hand". Nothing below is from memory.

Our numbers for reference: interval-scale Krippendorff alpha / ICC(1) about 0.45 overall, 0.50 methods, 0.2-0.3 advancing knowledge and logic/communication, 0.06 open science, 0.3-0.4 journal-tier predictions; median gap between two evaluators about 10 percentile points; evaluators' 90% intervals contain the co-evaluator's midpoint 51% of the time vs about 70% expected.

## Comparability caveats (apply throughout)

- Most of the literature reports single-rater reliability on a coarse recommendation (accept/revise/reject) or a 1-9 score. We use 0-100 percentile ratings with two raters per paper, so ICC(1) is a single-rater coefficient. Studies that report the correlation of committee-average scores (NeurIPS) are not single-rater and will look higher.
- Range restriction lowers agreement. Pier et al. (2018) used only well-scored NIH applications and found ICC about 0. Erosheva et al. (2021) show that when the full range of proposals is used, IRR is above 0.6 in NIH and AIBS data, and that zero estimates in restricted subsets are expected. Unjournal papers are pre-selected as "worth evaluating", so some range restriction applies to us too, but the spread of true quality in our sample is probably wider than in a "top-scoring only" sample.
- Kappa and ICC are not interchangeable. Bornmann et al. report both separately (ICC about .34, kappa about .17).

Comparison for us: our overall ICC of about 0.45 is higher than the typical single-reviewer coefficient for journal manuscripts (meta-analytic about 0.34; individual studies 0.17-0.25; economics and finance referees 0.15-0.25) and in the same range as the correlation between two committees' mean scores at NeurIPS (0.55-0.59, which averages several reviewers each and is therefore not single-rater). The methods (0.50) rating is as reliable as anything in the manuscript literature. The advancing-knowledge and logic/communication coefficients (0.2-0.3) are typical of the manuscript and grant evidence; open science at 0.06 is below anything I found, though I found no study of an equivalent item.

The grant literature's 0.2-0.3 matches our lower-reliability criteria, not our headline overall rating.

## Q3. Multi-dimensional criteria: do sub-criteria differ in reliability, and does the overall rating track particular criteria?

|---|---|---|---|

| Jirschitzka et al. 2017 (Scientometrics 113:1059) - read abstract | 443 reviews of 145 submissions to an interdisciplinary conference; rating dimensions such as relevance and soundness | Reliability "rather poor"; convergent and discriminant validity of the dimensions low; a multidimensional model fitted better than a unidimensional one; high novelty ratings from same-discipline reviewers were negatively associated with citations. No per-dimension coefficients in the abstract. | 130 reviewers |
| Abdoul et al. 2012 (PLoS ONE) - read | Qualitative study of French and other grant criteria | One reviewer describes halo directly: reviewers form an overall opinion and then assign the scores and subscores to match. This is an interview quote, not a tested effect. Reviewers weight criteria differently (originality, methodology, feasibility). | 65 reviewers interviewed; 14 funders' calls |
| Pier et al. 2018 (PNAS) - read | NIH preliminary ratings, number of strengths, number of weaknesses | ICC 0 for ratings, 0 for strengths, 0.017 for weaknesses | 25 applications |

Comparison for us: the pattern that a methods or "approach" criterion is both the most reliable and the strongest driver of the overall score (our methods 0.50 vs overall 0.45) matches NIH, where approach has by far the largest weight and correlation with the overall score (0.84). Lower reliability for the less central criteria (significance/innovation analogues, environment lowest at .35 test-retest) matches our advancing-knowledge and logic/communication results. I found no study that reports inter-rater reliability separately by criterion for journal manuscripts or for an open-science item, so the 0.06 open-science figure has no direct benchmark. Halo is documented only qualitatively; the high inter-criterion correlations (0.55-0.75) at NIH are consistent with it but do not prove it.

## Q4. Overconfidence of expert and lay interval estimates

All hit rates below are the share of true values falling inside the stated interval, against the nominal level.

| Source | What was measured | Hit rate | Sample |
|---|---|---|---|
| McKenzie, Liersch & Yaniv 2008 (OBHDP 107:179) - read | 90% intervals from IT professionals and UCSD students, own-domain (expert) vs other-domain | 41% overall (bounded questions) and 39% (unbounded); experts 44% vs novices 41%. Abstract: 90% intervals "typically" contain the truth about 50% of the time. Introduction: "between 30% and 60%" across studies; Russo & Schoemaker 1992 professionals' 90%/95% intervals had hit rates of 40-60% | Experiment 1 of that paper (about 136 students plus IT professionals) |
| Moore & Healy 2008 (Psychological Review 115:502) - read | Review statement and own task | "90% confidence intervals contain the correct answer less than 50% of the time" (cites Alpert & Raiffa 1982; Klayman et al. 1999; Soll & Klayman 2004); prior findings 30-50% for 90% intervals; their own quiz-score task: 90.5% confidence, 73.1% hit | 82 students |
| Soll & Klayman 2004 (JEP:LMC 30:299-314) - read abstract (Duke page) | High/low estimates for X% intervals, general-knowledge items | "Substantial overconfidence"; intervals in some cases only about 40% as wide as needed; strongly dependent on elicitation format. Actual hit-rate figures not retrieved. | n/a |
| Alpert & Raiffa 1982 (in Kahneman, Slovic & Tversky eds.) - second-hand only | 50% interquartile intervals on ten quantities | Block & Harper (1991) describe the finding as about 33% of 50% intervals containing the truth. 98% interval figures differ across secondary accounts, so none quoted. | n/a |
| Morgan 2014 (PNAS 111:7176) - read via fetch summary | Surprise index (share of true values outside an assessor's 98% interval; ideal 2%) | 21 studies, over 10,000 questions: none reached 2%, over half had 30% or more outside | 21 studies |
| Speirs-Bridge et al. 2010 (Risk Analysis 30:512) - read abstract | Experts (epidemiologists, public health, ecologists), 80% intervals, three-point vs four-step elicitation | Average hit rate 68.1% for 80% intervals across three studies (overconfidence about 11.9 points), described as a substantial reduction vs earlier studies; four-step format better (d = 0.61, CI 0.04-1.18) | 79 experts |
| Park & Budescu 2015 (JDM 10:130) - read via fetch summary, second-hand for source data | Individual 90% intervals in prior datasets (Glaser, Langer & Weber data) | General knowledge about 33%; economics/finance questions about 22% | individual intervals, k=1 |
| Ben-David, Graham & Harvey 2013 - second-hand (Duke listing via search summary; journal not confirmed) | CFOs' 80% intervals for next-year S&P 500 return | Realized return inside the interval 36% of the time (working-paper versions 33% and 38%) | over 13,300 forecasts, 10-year panel |
| Lin & Bier 2008 (Reliability Engineering & System Safety 93:711) - read abstract | Cooke/TU Delft database of experts' 90% intervals | Abstract gives no headline hit rate; about 5,000 intervals; overconfidence varies significantly between studies, experts and questions | about 5,000 intervals |
| Cooke classical-model data | Experts' 5-95% bands | I could not verify a hit-rate figure; a search summary quoted 36% for post-2006 studies but I could not trace it to a paper. Do not use without checking Cooke & Goossens 2008 and Colson & Cooke 2017 directly. | n/a |

Comparison for us: stated 90% intervals typically contain the truth 30-60% of the time, in experts as well as novices (McKenzie et al. find experts 44% vs novices 41%); well-designed expert elicitation gets to about 68% for 80% intervals. Our 51% against about 70% expected is a different test (the target is a noisy co-evaluator midpoint, and the benchmark is already adjusted), but the implied ratio, intervals about half as wide as warranted, is in line with Soll & Klayman's "as little as 40% as wide as needed". So our evaluators look about as overconfident as the standard literature predicts, not unusually so.

## Q5. Percentile / reference-class framing, anchoring and calibration training

| Source | What was measured | Number | Sample |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tamblyn et al. 2023 (PLoS ONE) - read abstract | Ranking applications relative to others vs rating them on a scale | ICC: phase 1 ranking 0.59 vs rating 0.54; phase 2 ranking 0.38 vs rating 0.25; ranking less affected by reviewer expertise and experience, and sex bias appeared only with rating | 3,156 CIHR applications |
| Sattler et al. 2015 (PLoS ONE 10:e0130450) - read abstract | Randomized trial: 11-minute training video on the NIH 9-point scale vs none, rating four proposal summaries | ICC(2,k) 0.89 (CI 0.71-0.99) with training vs 0.61 (CI 0.32-0.96) without; 74% vs 35% of ratings "correct" | 75 public health professors; vignettes, not real proposals (authors: best case) |
| Pier et al. 2017 (Research Evaluation) - read abstract | Panel discussion and score calibration talk | Within-panel agreement rises after discussion; between-panel agreement falls | 4 mock NIH panels |
| Pier et al. 2018 (PNAS) - read | Whether reviewers differ in translating strengths and weaknesses into a score | Reviewers disagreed on how to translate a given number of strengths/weaknesses into a rating, despite identical instructions | 43 reviewers |
| Wang & Shah 2019 (AAMAS; arXiv:1806.05085) - read summary only | Theory: arbitrary reviewer scale miscalibration | Shows cardinal scores can still beat ranking-only estimators under arbitrary but ranking-consistent miscalibration (no empirical agreement numbers) | n/a |
| Mellers et al. 2014 (Psychological Science 25:1106) - abstract via search; challenged by Holden et al. 2025 | Forecaster training including reference-class use | Reported gains in calibration and resolution; a 2025 reanalysis disputes the size of the training effect | IARPA tournament, 2,246 first-year participants |
| Speirs-Bridge et al. 2010 | Elicitation format | See Q4: a staged interval format raised hit rate to about 68% for 80% intervals | 79 experts |

I found no study that evaluates percentile-scale or explicit reference-class ratings in manuscript peer review; the closest are ranking-vs-rating (Tamblyn) and scale-training (Sattler). Comparison for us: relative framing and short anchoring training have the best available evidence of raising agreement, but the training evidence is from vignettes, and the ranking gain at CIHR is modest (0.05 to 0.13 ICC points). Our percentile framing is not itself evidence of unusually high agreement; panel discussion (Pier 2017) is a reminder that making evaluators converge is different from making them reliable.

Comparison for us: at ICLR, LLM-to-human score correlations (about 0.12-0.26) are no lower than human-to-human (0.14-0.18) in the same pre-rebuttal data, while LLMs agree with each other far more (0.49). That is the benchmark to use if the report compares an LLM-evaluator against our human pairs: our human-human coefficient (0.45) is much higher than ICLR's, so an LLM matching only 0.1-0.3 correlation with a human would be well below our human-human level. I did not find any study on LLM ratings of economics papers against expert percentile ratings.
